## What wears out first

- **Surface texture.** The grit on a carbon or textured face wears down with use. Spin usually drops first, and many players notice it before anything else.
- **The edge guard.** Scraping the paddle on the court cracks, chips or peels it. This is common on paddles used for a lot of defensive and low shots.
- **The core and face bond.** Over time the core can break down, or the face can separate from it in places. That produces dead spots and a change in sound.
- **The grip.** The cheapest part to fix. Replace the overgrip when it gets slick.

## Signs it is time to replace your paddle

- The sound changes. A duller, more hollow or rattling sound often points to a problem inside the paddle.
- You find dead spots, areas of the face where the ball loses pop.
- The face feels smooth, and your spin has dropped.
- You see cracks in the edge guard, or bubbles or ripples on the face.
- You are adjusting your game to make up for the paddle.

## How long, roughly?

Players who play several times a week often report a noticeable change in pop or spin within a year. Casual players can get much longer out of one paddle. Treat any single number you read with suspicion.

## How to make a paddle last longer

- Do not leave it in a hot car. Heat is hard on the glue between the face and the core.
- Keep it in a cover or bag between sessions.
- Avoid scraping the edge on the court, and do not use the paddle to pick up balls.
- Wipe the face with a damp cloth. Skip harsh cleaners.
- Rotate two paddles if you play most days.

## Buying with durability in mind

A more expensive paddle is not automatically more durable. Look for owner reviews that describe how a paddle held up after months of play, not first impressions. See [how to choose a pickleball paddle]({{< relref "/guides/how-to-choose-a-pickleball-paddle" >}}) for the rest of the checklist.
