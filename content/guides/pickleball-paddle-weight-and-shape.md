## Weight ranges

Brands draw the lines in slightly different places. As a rough guide:

| Class | Rough weight | Usually means |
|---|---|---|
| Light | Under about 7.5 oz | Fast hands, less tiring to swing, less stability and power |
| Mid | About 7.5 to 8.5 oz | The balance most players end up with |
| Heavy | Over about 8.5 oz | More power and stability, slower hands, more tiring over a long session |

Weight cuts both ways for your arm. A light paddle is less work to swing, but a heavier one is more stable and sends less shock up your arm on off-center hits. If you have elbow or shoulder problems, talk to a coach or doctor before you make a big change.

## Balance matters as much as weight

Two paddles with the same weight can swing very differently. A head-heavy paddle puts more mass toward the tip, which adds power and makes the paddle feel heavier in the swing. A head-light paddle feels quicker and easier to control at the net. Some brands publish swing weight, which tells you more about how a paddle swings than static weight does.

## Shape

| Shape | Usually means |
|---|---|
| Standard | Balanced sweet spot and reach. A good default. |
| Elongated | More reach and leverage for power, a narrower sweet spot |
| Widebody | A bigger sweet spot and more forgiveness, less reach |

Under USA Pickleball rules, a paddle's length and width together can be no more than 24 inches, and its length can be no more than 17 inches. That caps how extreme a shape can get.

## Grip size

Most grips measure between about 4 and 4.5 inches around. A quick check: hold the paddle in an eastern (handshake) grip and slide the index finger of your other hand into the gap between the tip of your ring finger and the base of your palm. The finger should fit snugly. If it will not fit, the grip is too small. If there is a lot of room, it is too large. You can build a grip up with an overgrip, but you cannot easily make a big one smaller.
