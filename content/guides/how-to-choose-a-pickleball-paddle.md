## 1. Start with how you play

Doubles players spend most of their time dinking, dropping and resetting at the kitchen line, so touch and control matter most. Singles players cover more court and often want reach and power. Be honest about your own game. If your shots keep sailing long, you probably want more control. If your drives land short and soft, you probably want more pop.

## 2. Pick a weight you can swing all session

Heavier paddles bring power and stability. Lighter paddles are quicker at the net and less tiring to swing. Where the weight sits matters as much as the number on the label. Our [weight and shape guide]({{< relref "/guides/pickleball-paddle-weight-and-shape" >}}) goes through both.

## 3. Choose a core thickness

Thicker cores (16mm) generally feel softer and more controlled. Thinner cores (13mm) generally feel livelier. Read the [13mm vs 14mm vs 16mm guide]({{< relref "/guides/pickleball-paddle-thickness" >}}) before you decide, because the face material and the build can change the feel as much as the thickness does.

## 4. Look at the face material

Carbon fiber faces tend to give more control and spin. Fiberglass faces tend to give more pop. Surface texture also wears, which affects spin over time. See [carbon fiber vs fiberglass]({{< relref "/guides/carbon-fiber-vs-fiberglass-pickleball-paddles" >}}).

## 5. Check shape and grip size

Elongated paddles give reach and a smaller sweet spot. Wider paddles are more forgiving. A grip that is too big or too small costs you control and can strain your forearm. Both are covered in the [weight and shape guide]({{< relref "/guides/pickleball-paddle-weight-and-shape" >}}).

## 6. Check the paddle is approved if you need it

If you play sanctioned tournaments or leagues, check the paddle against the current USA Pickleball approved list, or your league's own list. For regular rec play, most people never get asked.

## 7. Set a budget, and plan to replace it

Paddles run from well under $100 to well over $200. A higher price usually buys better materials and tighter manufacturing. It does not buy skill. A mid-priced paddle that suits you beats a premium paddle that does not. Paddles also wear out, so factor in replacement. See [how long pickleball paddles last]({{< relref "/guides/how-long-do-pickleball-paddles-last" >}}).

## Quick reference

These are general tendencies, not rules. Construction varies a lot between paddles.

| If you want | Look for |
|---|---|
| More control and touch | 14 to 16mm core, carbon fiber face |
| More power | 13 to 14mm core, heavier or elongated paddle |
| More forgiveness | Wider face, thicker core |
| Less arm strain | Softer, thicker core, correct grip size, a weight you can swing without forcing it |
| More spin | Textured carbon fiber face (and expect it to wear) |

## Demo first, and read the right reviews

If you can, hit with a friend's paddle or a demo before you buy. When you read reviews, look for people who have owned the paddle for months, not days.
